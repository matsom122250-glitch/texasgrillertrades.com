Research note · Published Nov 17, 2026

Central banks keep buying gold. Why is the price still falling?

The world's strongest commercial banks aren't piling into gold. They hold cash and government bonds. The big gold buyers are central banks, led by China, and they're buying as insurance. But they're only about a quarter of demand, and right now traders reacting to rate hikes, 5% Treasury yields and a strong dollar are setting the price.

1. What counts as the "strongest" bank?

It depends on what you measure. The three best-known rankings use different yardsticks and come out with very different lists.

Among privately owned banks, JPMorgan is generally considered the strongest all-around. That's a common market view, not a formal ranking.

2. What the strongest banks actually hold

Commercial banks don't stockpile gold. Their balance sheets are loans, government bonds and cash.

  • China's state banks are buying their own government's bonds. Loan demand is weak. By July, claims on the government were 16.4% of Chinese bank assets, up from 11.5% five years earlier, and for the first time larger than loans to households. At Agricultural Bank of China, bonds drove over 90% of first-half interest income growth.
  • JPMorgan is sitting on cash and securities. Jamie Dimon said on the Q3 call that the bank holds about $1.5 trillion in cash and marketable securities, plus roughly $30 billion of excess capital it would rather lend "at very good returns" than spend on buybacks.
  • Gold at commercial banks is client business. JPMorgan, HSBC and a few others are "bullion banks" that vault, trade and lend gold for clients and central banks. Those positions are mostly hedged, not a bet on the price.

3. Who is buying gold: central banks, as insurance

In the World Gold Council's 2026 survey of 76 central banks, 90% said gold's performance in a crisis is a reason to hold it, a record. 84% cited it as a store of value and 83% as a diversifier. 74% expect to hold fewer U.S. dollars five years from now.

The risks they're insuring against:

  • Sanctions. After the West froze Russia's reserves in 2022, gold's appeal grew because it is "no one's liability," as a Bank of Italy official put it this fall. Gold in your own vault can't be frozen by another government.
  • Geopolitics. 85% of emerging-market central banks call gold a geopolitical hedge, versus 56% of advanced-economy ones. The survey found geopolitics overtook inflation as a concern this year.
  • Too much reliance on the dollar. This is diversification, not dumping. Poland says it wants a "more diversified reserve structure," not to replace dollars and euros. UBS calls the mood "dissatisfaction without displacement."

4. So why is the price falling?

Central banks bought 42% more than a year ago, and total demand still fell 1%. Everyone else pulled back.

The day-to-day price is set mostly by traders in futures and other financial markets, not by physical buyers. This month, those traders are reacting to interest rates:

Part of this is January unwinding. Gold ran to about $5,600 in late January on war fears and speculative buying. As peace hopes in the Middle East came and went, some of that fear premium came out. Even after the drop, gold is still well above where it traded two years ago; this is a big correction from a spike, not a collapse.

What to watch

  • The Fed's December meeting. A hike is about 90% priced in. A pause would likely help gold.
  • Q4 central bank data (out early next year). It will show whether central banks slowed down or bought the dip. Our numbers run only through September.
  • The dollar and the 10-year yield. A dollar near a 19-month high and yields above 5% are the biggest headwinds.
  • Physical buyers. There are early reports that lower prices are bringing jewelry and coin buyers back in some markets.

Bottom line

The world's strongest banks are holding cash and government bonds, not gold. Central banks, led by China and Poland, are buying gold as insurance against sanctions, geopolitics and dependence on the dollar. But they're about a quarter of demand. Jewelry and coin buyers have pulled back, and traders facing 5% Treasury yields, a strong dollar and likely Fed hikes are setting the price, and right now they're selling.
