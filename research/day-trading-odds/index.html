Day trading: the real odds, how the few win, and who they are

Day trading looks easy on social media. The research says otherwise: in the biggest studies, nearly everyone who sticks with it loses money, and fewer than 1 in 100 beat the market year after year. Here's what the data shows, what the rare winners do differently, and why the famous names are the exception that proves the rule.

What day trading is

Day trading means buying and selling the same stock, option, future or currency within a single day, trying to profit from small price moves. Day traders don't hold anything overnight. They make many trades, often with borrowed money (margin), and each trade usually aims for a small gain.

The odds, by the numbers

The best evidence comes from countries where researchers could see every trade by every individual, not just the success stories people choose to share.

  • Brazil (2013–2015 starters). Of 19,646 people who began day trading mini-index futures, 1,551 stuck with it for more than 300 days. 97% of them lost money. Only 17 earned more than the minimum wage, and 8 earned more than a bank teller's starting salary. The authors' conclusion: "it is virtually impossible for an individual to day trade for a living."
  • Taiwan (1992–2006). Using every trade on the Taiwan Stock Exchange, researchers found about 360,000 people day traded in a typical year. Fewer than 1% (about 1,000) could beat the market consistently, though roughly 13–15% were profitable after a reasonable allowance for trading costs.
  • Europe. When EU regulators restricted contracts for difference (CFDs) in 2018, they found 74–89% of retail accounts typically lost money.

Why the odds are so bad

  • It's close to a zero-sum game, minus costs. For every short-term winner there's a loser. Commissions, the bid-ask spread and taxes on short-term gains come out of every trade, win or lose. Making many trades multiplies those costs.
  • You're competing with professionals. On the other side of most trades are firms with faster computers, better data and teams of quants.
  • Leverage cuts both ways. Margin makes wins bigger and losses bigger. A few bad days can wipe out months of gains.
  • Human nature. Overconfidence after wins, holding losers too long, and "revenge trading" to win money back are some of the most documented mistakes in finance.
  • Losers keep going. The Taiwan researchers found many traders kept day trading even after years of losses, suggesting they overestimate their own skill.
What changed this year: the SEC approved FINRA's plan to scrap the $25,000 "pattern day trader" minimum on April 14, 2026. The new rules took effect June 4, and brokers have until October 20, 2027 to switch over. Margin accounts with more than $2,000 now get intraday buying power based on their positions. Day trading is easier to start. The odds haven't changed.

How the few succeed

There's no proven formula, but the traders who last, and the researchers who study them, keep pointing to the same habits:

  • Treat it as a business, not a hobby. Keep a written plan, track every trade, and measure results after all costs.
  • Risk very little per trade. Many professionals risk around 1% of their account on any single trade, so no one loss can sink them.
  • Have a real, tested edge. Know exactly why a setup should work, and test it on past data and with tiny positions before trusting it.
  • Specialize. The survivors tend to master a few markets or patterns instead of chasing whatever is moving.
  • Set a quit rule in advance. Decide up front how long you'll try and how much you'll lose before you stop. Most people never do, and that's where the big losses come from.
  • Be honest about the alternative. For most people, a low-cost index fund held for years has beaten day trading by a wide margin.

A word of caution about "guru" results: in 2022, the FTC said day-trading course seller Warrior Trading and its CEO Ross Cameron used deceptive earnings claims, and that "most customer accounts actually lost money." The company agreed to a $3 million settlement. If someone is selling a course, ask for audited results from typical customers, not just the founder's best trades.

Bottom line

Day trading can make money, but the evidence says very few people do it consistently. Roughly 97 out of 100 who stick with it lose, and fewer than 1 in 100 beat the market year after year. The few winners treat it like a business, risk very little per trade, measure everything after costs, and know when to quit. And with the $25,000 rule gone, more people will be learning that the hard way.
