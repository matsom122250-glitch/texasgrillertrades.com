Short selling, explained with stick figures

Most investors buy low and sell high. Short sellers do it backwards: they sell high first, then buy low later. It's a bet that a stock will fall. Here's how it works, why it can go badly wrong, and why Wall Street does it anyway.

The idea in one sentence

You borrow a share you don't own, sell it today, and hope to buy it back cheaper later so you can return it and pocket the difference.

When it works

Meet Shorty. Shorty thinks Banana Corp (a made-up company) is overpriced at $100 a share.

StepWhat happensShorty's cash
1. BorrowBroker lends 1 share$0
2. SellSells it for $100+$100
3. WaitPrice falls to $60+$100
4. Buy back and returnPays $60, gives the share back+$40, minus fees

When it goes wrong

Same trade, but this time Banana Corp goes up.

This is the part that surprises people. When you buy a stock, the worst case is it goes to $0 and you lose what you paid. When you short a stock, there's no ceiling on how high it can go, so there's no limit on what you can lose.

A real short squeeze: in January 2021, GameStop was one of the most heavily shorted stocks in the market. Retail traders piled in, short sellers rushed to buy back, and the stock went from under $20 at the start of the month to an intraday high of $483 by January 28. Some hedge funds lost billions.

The hidden costs

  • Borrowing fee. You pay interest to borrow the shares. For popular shorts it can be very expensive.
  • Dividends. If the company pays a dividend while you're short, you owe it to the lender.
  • Margin. You need a margin account and must keep enough cash in it. If the stock rises, the broker can demand more or close your trade at a loss.
  • Timing. You can be right that a stock is overpriced and still lose money if it keeps rising for months first.

So why does anyone do it?

  • Hedging. A fund that owns lots of stocks may short an index to soften the blow if the whole market drops.
  • Betting against bad companies. Short sellers dig into companies they think are overvalued, badly run or dishonest. Some have exposed real frauds, including Enron and Wirecard.
  • Keeping markets running. Market makers short shares all day to fill customers' buy orders.

Critics argue short sellers can pile on and push struggling companies down further. Defenders say they find problems everyone else misses and help keep prices honest. Both happen.

Bottom line

Short selling is selling first and buying later. If the price falls, you win. If it rises, you lose, and the losses have no limit. That's why it's mostly a tool for professionals and hedgers, and why the biggest short-selling stories are usually about squeezes.
